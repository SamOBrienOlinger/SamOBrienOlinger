The brief

The need behind the work.

Rights and professional decisions become easier to examine when the situation is visible. The challenge is to let a learner compare perspectives without losing context or suggesting that the two roles have equal powers and responsibilities.

My contribution

I developed the concept, research-informed scenarios and browser experience. The implementation connects the public and Garda perspectives within the same branching encounter, with explanations and supporting sources.

The thinking behind the build

Three decisions that shaped it.

01

One encounter, two sets of answers

Switching perspective keeps the scene, the character pair and both roles’ responses. A learner can compare the reasoning at the same moment instead of starting an unrelated lesson.

The active role’s answer determines the next branch. Shared context supports comparison while keeping the different responsibilities explicit.

02

Make feedback more useful than a score

Each choice leads to an explanation, a reason it matters and source links. Recaps, retrying an answer and exploring the other side support revisiting the reasoning.

Scores help track learning points. They do not measure personal bias or certify professional competence.

03

Keep the first step simple

A suggested situation can start immediately. Choosing another character and browsing all situations are optional. There is no sign-up and no timer.

Progress can be saved in the current browser. Specialist legal, community, accessibility and language review remain necessary as the prototype develops.

From idea to delivery

What was delivered.

A working browser prototype with fourteen shared situations, public and Garda perspectives, branching decisions, role-specific feedback, recaps and locally saved progress.

What comes next

The next stage is specialist review and observed sessions with learners. Those checks should examine the clarity of the rights information, the treatment of each perspective and what learners take away.
